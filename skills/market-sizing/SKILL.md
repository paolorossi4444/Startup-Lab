# Market sizing

## Purpose
Produce a number that survives being challenged line by line, and answer the question the
number exists for: is the share we would need plausible?

## Inputs
- **Required:** what is sold, to whom, where, and what decision the number has to support.
- **Also read if provided:** expert call notes, filings, prior analyses, the company's own
  pricing and customer list.
- **Geography:** split it the way the market splits, not by habit. Say which figure covers
  which region, and which regions are excluded and why.

## Choosing the structure
There is no standard layout. Work out the right one before building, and say which choices
you made and why. Three decisions settle it:

1. **The unit of demand.** What is actually bought: a workload, a seat, a contract, a
   transaction, a vehicle, a site. Everything else follows from this.
2. **The cut.** Segment by what drives value *and* is reported in the data — spend category,
   industry, company size band, region, customer type, or a combination. Differentiate the
   value per customer across bands rather than applying one average to all of them.
3. **The layers.** TAM alone, TAM and SAM, or down to SOM. Compute what the decision needs,
   state which layers you produced, and say why the others were not required.

Two reasoning patterns, as logic to adapt rather than templates to copy:

- **When the market is already measured as spend**, start top-down from a named forecast and
  judge it category by category: what is addressable, what is not, and what share of each
  category can actually be captured given who else takes that revenue. Then build bottom-up
  from unit economics against a counted population of buyers to see whether the two meet.
- **When the market is a population of buyers**, start bottom-up: count firms by industry and
  size band from official registers, build the annual value per customer from its drivers for
  each band, and grid it by geography. Then find published spend figures covering the same
  perimeter and work down to check it from above.

**Both builds are always produced**, whichever one leads. A sizing with only one of them is
incomplete, because neither method catches its own errors.

## 1. Define the perimeter before any number
One paragraph: what counts as in this market and what does not, and on what basis. State the
ICP threshold explicitly where one exists — company size, revenue, licence, fleet, volume —
because it decides who gets counted. Every later figure refers to this definition. A sizing
without a stated perimeter cannot be checked.

## 2. Choose and verify the sources
Source selection is part of the work, not a given. For this specific market, work out which
bodies actually hold the data, then verify each one:

- Who counts the buyers: statistical offices, business registers, chambers of commerce,
  census bureaus, sector regulators, licensing bodies
- Who tracks the spend: regulators, trade associations, central banks, industry bodies,
  named analyst forecasts
- Who reveals the economics: filed accounts, listed-company disclosures, prospectuses and
  S-1s of comparable companies, public tenders and framework contracts
- Who knows what is not published: practitioners in the market, for the judgment inputs

For every source, record the dataset name and identifier, the publisher, the last update
date, the scope of the universe it covers, what it excludes, and the link. Open the original.
Never carry a figure second-hand: if you have it only through someone else's report, say so
and name who reported it.

## 3. Build bottom-up
1. **Unit economics first.** Build the annual value per customer from its drivers — price per
   seat or per unit, usage, frequency, plus fixed components — rather than assuming an average
   contract value. Show the formula. Where value differs by band, build it per band.
2. **Count the buyers** by segment, taking counts from the sources above rather than typing
   them in, so every cell is traceable.
3. **Grid it** by segment and geography, with the same columns in each block: buyers, value
   per buyer, market, relevance share, addressable market.
4. **Regional adjustments** go in one visible input each, referenced by the rows they affect.
5. **Totals** carry a weighted average value per buyer, and any blended percentage is computed
   from the totals, never assumed.

## 4. Build top-down
1. Start from a named third-party forecast, with publisher and date, kept in full.
2. For each category, mark whether it is addressable and **write the reason in its own
   column**: which product serves it, or why nothing does.
3. Break addressable categories into sub-segments where the split changes the answer.
4. Apply a capture rate per category, each with its written rationale — who else takes that
   revenue, and why this share remains.
5. Apply the filters that define the perimeter, one visible input each.
6. Apply the geographic split, stating which regions are in and which are out.
7. Present it as a waterfall: total market, less what is not addressable, relevant pools,
   times each filter, global addressable market, less excluded regions, final figure — each
   line carrying its methodology note.

## 5. Core and adjacent markets
Where the company can plausibly sell beyond its first market, size the two separately and
label them. The core is where it sells today. An adjacent market enters the arithmetic only
with evidence of pull — pilots, inbound demand, conversations already happening — named next
to it. An adjacency supported by plausibility alone is mentioned in words and left out of the
numbers.

## 6. Evidence the judgment inputs
Every percentage that is a judgment sits in its own cell with a written justification.
Judgment inputs are necessary and legitimate; unexplained rules of thumb are not. Where the
number comes from practitioners, quote them: the words, the role, the organisation. Prefer
several independent voices that converge over one confident one, and show the range they
give. Where no evidence exists, label the input an assumption and carry it into the
sensitivity.

## 7. Reconcile the two builds
Report both numbers and the gap between them in percent, then explain the gap through the
inputs that cause it. If they differ by more than 3x, say which one you trust and why. A
projection may blend the two, but only after the gap has been explained.

## 8. What moves this market, for and against
A size is a snapshot; the decision depends on direction. Set out the forces on both sides, and
never only the favourable ones.

**Drivers** — what increases demand. For each: what it is, the evidence it is real with a date
and source, how large the effect is where it can be quantified, and when it acts — already
under way, within one to three years, or later. Say whether it is structural, and therefore
durable, or cyclical, and therefore temporary. A budget rising because of a one-off programme
is not the same as a cost falling permanently.

**Headwinds** — what reduces, delays or caps demand, held to the same standard: regulation that
blocks or slows adoption, procurement cycles, substitution by something cheaper, saturation
where the obvious buyers are already served, falling prices that shrink the pool even as volumes
rise, macro conditions that postpone discretionary spend, or a dependency outside the market's
control.

**The net, and the growth rate.** State how these forces combine and over what period. Then do
the thing that makes this section worth writing: show that the growth rate used in the sizing
follows from the named drivers. A rate taken from a published forecast and left unexplained is
somebody else's assumption carried into your model; if the drivers cannot account for it, say so
and use a rate you can defend.

**What would reverse it.** The specific event or change that would turn the direction around,
and how visible it would be before it happened.

## 9. Cross-check outside your own model
Compare against other published sizings of the same market, and against the disclosures of
comparable companies — prospectuses, S-1s and annual reports often carry a sized market and
real unit economics. Report where you sit against them, including when you are the outlier.

Then run at least three internal checks and report them even when they pass:
- Does the market exceed the total spend of the sector it sits inside?
- Is the implied spend per buyer plausible against that buyer's total budget?
- Does the implied revenue of the largest existing player match its reported figures?

## 10. The implied-share test
A market size on its own decides nothing. Work back from the outcome being tested: target
exit value, multiple, margin, implied revenue, and therefore the market share required. State
whether that share is plausible, measured against what leaders in comparable markets actually
hold.

## 11. Sensitivity
Name the two or three inputs that move the result most, and show the result when each moves
to a pessimistic and an optimistic value. Give the answer as a range.

## Output
Open with the findings as statements with the numbers inside them — what the market is worth
on each definition, which way it is moving and why, what share would be needed, and whether
that is plausible — then the tables, then the method. Round honestly: no three significant
figures on a number built from judgment inputs.

The drivers and headwinds go next to the growth figure they explain, not in a separate chapter
at the end, so the reader sees the force and the number together.

## Deliverable
Two files.

**The model (.xlsx)** — built with the xlsx skill, laid out so another analyst can follow it:
a cover with subject, date and author; an output tab carrying both builds, the projection and
the implied share; one tab per method; then the raw sources, one tab each, pasted as pulled
with their dataset header rather than retyped; superseded versions kept rather than deleted.
Inputs and judgment cells sit apart from calculations, and every judgment cell carries its
rationale in an adjacent column.

**The memo (.docx)** — built with the docx skill: findings, tables, method, sources.

In the chat: the headline numbers, the implied share and the sensitivity only.

## Rules
- Always build both bottom-up and top-down, independently, before comparing them. Never tune
  one to match the other.
- Define the perimeter and the ICP threshold before producing any number.
- Identify and verify the sources for this specific market. Never reuse a source list by
  habit, and never cite a dataset you have not opened.
- No figure without its source and date, or an open label saying it is an assumption.
- Every judgment percentage carries its evidence or its rationale in writing.
- Report the checks that fail. A failed check is the most useful line in the analysis.
- Never invent a source, a figure, a quote or a link. If a number cannot be found, say what is
  missing and what it would take to get it.
- Show that the growth rate follows from the named drivers. A rate inherited from a published
  forecast without explanation is not defensible.
- Report headwinds at the same length and with the same evidence as drivers. A market with only
  tailwinds was not researched.
- Give a range, not a single confident number, whenever the inputs are judgments.
