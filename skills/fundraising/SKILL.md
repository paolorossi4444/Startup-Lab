# Fundraising

## Purpose
Decide how much to raise and on what terms, with every figure derived from what the money has
to achieve rather than chosen because it sounds right.

## Inputs
- **Required:** the current cash position, the burn, and what the company is trying to reach.
- **Read first where it exists:** the financial model, which carries the burn path, the runway
  and the milestone cost. Without it, build the burn path here, roughly, and say that it is
  rough.
- **Also useful:** the existing cap table, any prior instruments outstanding, and what has
  already been promised to anyone.

## How to reason
A round is a purchase: the founder sells a share of the company to buy the time and resources
to reach a specific position. Everything follows from what that position is and what it costs
to get there.

- **The amount is derived, never chosen.** Milestone, cost to reach it, buffer. A number that
  cannot be reconstructed backwards is a guess that an investor will treat as one.
- **Dilution is a price, not an insult.** The question is not how little to give away but what
  the company is worth more with than without.
- **Valuation is set by ownership arithmetic, not by what the company is worth today.** Most
  early rounds are priced by how much the investor needs to own and how much the founder needs
  to raise.
- **Price is what founders negotiate; terms are what costs them.** A high valuation with a
  participating preference is worse than a lower one without it in most outcomes.
- **Benchmarks move fast.** Search for current figures and cite them. Never quote a valuation
  or ownership benchmark from memory.

## 1. What the round has to buy
Name the position the company must reach before the next raise: the evidence the next investor
will require, not a date. Typically this is revenue at a level, retention proven over a period,
a product shipped and in use, a regulatory approval, or a repeatable channel.

Then say what that position requires: which hires, what spend, how long. This is the whole
argument for the amount, and it comes from the financial model.

## 2. The amount, derived
Build it, and show the build:

1. **Monthly burn** on the plan that reaches the milestone, rising as hires land.
2. **Months needed** to reach the milestone, with the slippage the plan should expect.
3. **Plus the time to raise the next round**, six months at least, because the next raise
   starts well before the money runs out.
4. **Plus any one-off costs** the milestone requires: equipment, deposits, licences, a first
   production run.
5. **A buffer**, stated as a buffer and sized to the risk of the plan.

The output is a specific figure. If the arithmetic gives 640,000 then the ask is 640,000, or
700,000 with the extra named as additional buffer. A round number that nobody can reconstruct
invites the question of whether the plan behind it exists.

Say in one sentence what the money buys: the months it covers, the milestone it reaches, and
what is true at the end of it that is not true now.

## 3. Use of funds
Split the amount across hires, product, acquisition, infrastructure and overheads, with the
share each takes and what each delivers. An investor reads this to see whether the plan matches
the ask; a founder uses it later to know when they have gone off the plan.

## 4. The instrument
- **Priced equity round**: a valuation is agreed, shares are issued, the cap table changes now.
  Slower, more expensive in legal fees, and definitive.
- **Convertible instrument** (a SAFE or convertible note): money now, conversion into equity at
  the next priced round, governed by a valuation cap, a discount, or both. Faster, but dilution
  is deferred rather than avoided.

For convertibles, the mechanics that decide the outcome: whether the cap is on the pre-money or
post-money basis, since a post-money instrument fixes the investor's percentage and puts all
subsequent dilution on the founders; the discount; pro-rata rights; and most-favoured-nation
clauses. Where several instruments are stacked, model what they convert into together, because
founders routinely discover the total only at conversion.

## 5. How the valuation is set
Three forces, in this order of practical weight:

1. **The investor's ownership requirement.** A fund needs a certain share for the position to
   matter to its portfolio. That requirement and the amount raised together imply the
   valuation, which is why the arithmetic usually runs backwards from ownership.
2. **Comparable rounds** at the same stage, sector and geography, recent. Search for these and
   cite them with dates; they move quickly and a figure from eighteen months ago can be wrong
   by a factor.
3. **What the company has proven.** Evidence raises the valuation within the range that the
   first two set; it rarely moves the company out of that range.

Show the relationship explicitly: at this amount and this ownership, the implied post-money is
this, and the pre-money is that. Then say whether that is consistent with what comparable
companies raised, and if not, why.

## 6. Dilution and the cap table
Model it rather than describing it:

- The cap table today, fully diluted.
- The round: new shares, the investor's resulting percentage, everyone else's new percentage.
- **The option pool.** Where the pool is created or increased before the round, it comes out of
  the pre-money and dilutes the existing holders alone, which is to say the founders. A pool
  created after the round dilutes everyone. This single choice moves founder ownership by
  several points without ever appearing in the headline price, and it is reported explicitly.
- Any convertibles converting at this round, with their caps and discounts applied.

## 7. Where this leads
Carry the cap table through the plausible next rounds with typical dilution at each, and show
founder ownership at each stage and at an exit. The point is not precision, which is impossible,
but the shape: whether the path leaves the founders with enough to stay motivated, and whether
this round's terms make the next one harder.

## 8. The terms that matter more than price
For each, say what it means and what it costs in a bad outcome as well as a good one:

- **Liquidation preference**: its multiple, and whether it is participating. Non-participating
  at one times is the common standard; participating means the investor takes the preference
  and then shares what is left.
- **Anti-dilution**: full ratchet against broad-based weighted average, and what each does to
  the founders if a later round is priced lower.
- **Board composition and protective provisions**: who decides what, and which decisions need
  investor consent.
- **Founder vesting**: schedule, cliff, and what happens on a sale or a departure.
- **Pro-rata and information rights**: who can follow on and what they must be shown.

State plainly that this is not legal advice and that a term sheet needs a lawyer who does this
for a living. The purpose here is that the founder knows what they are agreeing to before the
lawyer explains it.

## 9. Running the process
How many investors to approach and in what order, how long it realistically takes from first
meeting to money in the bank, what a process looks like when it is working, and what to do when
it is not. Urgency comes from a real deadline and real parallel conversations; invented interest
is found out and costs more than the round.

## Output in the chat
- The amount, and the one sentence that justifies it
- What it buys: months, milestone, and what is true at the end
- The implied valuation range and the ownership it gives away
- Founder ownership after this round and after the next
- The single term that would most change the outcome

## Deliverable
**The cap table and dilution model (.xlsx)**, built with the xlsx skill: the table today, the
round with the option pool handled explicitly, any convertibles converting, and the path through
the next rounds, with the ask built up from burn and milestone on its own tab.

**The memo (.docx)**, built with the docx skill, named
`<company-slug>-fundraise-<YYYY-MM-DD>.docx`: the ask and its derivation, use of funds, the
instrument and why, the valuation logic with its comparables and sources, the dilution picture,
and the terms to watch.

## Rules
- Never propose a round number that the arithmetic does not produce. If the figure is rounded,
  say by how much and why.
- Always show the build from burn and milestone to amount. An ask with no build is not an ask.
- Search for current comparables and benchmarks and cite them with dates. Never state a
  valuation benchmark from memory.
- Always model the option pool explicitly and say which side of the round it sits on.
- Model stacked convertibles together, not one at a time.
- Report what each term costs in a bad outcome, not only in a good one.
- Say that this is not legal advice and that the term sheet needs a lawyer.
- Never suggest manufacturing competitive interest or a deadline that does not exist.
