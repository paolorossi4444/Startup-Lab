# Positioning

## Purpose
Find a claim that is true, that buyers care about, that rivals are not already making, and
that cannot be copied in a sprint.

## Inputs
- **Required:** what the product does and who it is for.
- **Also read if provided:** a competition analysis, with the criteria that decide the market
  and the homepage claims of rivals; interview notes, which supply the buyer's own words.
- **Missing:** ask at most two questions, then proceed with a stated choice.

## How to reason
Positioning is not a writing exercise. It is a claim about the buyer's world that has to be
true, differentiated and defensible — and the work is in finding it, not in phrasing it.

- **Start from the problem, not the product.** A position built on features is overtaken by
  the next release. A position built on a problem lasts as long as the problem does.
- **Use the buyer's words, not the industry's.** If the phrasing does not appear in interview
  notes or reviews, it is the founder's language, and buyers will not recognise themselves in
  it.
- **It must be true today.** A claim resting on the roadmap is a lie with a delay built in,
  and it collapses in the first demo. If the product cannot deliver it now, it is not the
  position yet.
- **Position against what is already said.** A claim that every competitor also makes is a
  requirement to be in the market, not a reason to be chosen.
- **Positioning is also what you refuse to say.** Narrowing is what makes a claim credible.
- **Judge by what a rival would have to do to match it**, not by how good it sounds.
- **Decide with evidence from buyers, not from internal agreement.** The founder and their
  friends are not the market.

## When the evidence is thin
Much of what follows may rest on nothing yet, and that is normal early. Where it does, the
answer gets shorter, not longer and more hedged. Say in the first line how much of this rests
on evidence and how much on reasoning. Reduce any unsupported section to its conclusion and the
one thing that would settle it. Put what to go and find out at the top rather than at the end.
A short honest answer is worth more than a long one padded to look complete.

## 1. One buyer at a time
Write for a single buyer in a single segment. Different buyers have different problems, and a
position written for all of them says nothing to any of them. Where several buyers matter,
write separate positions and say which is the lead.

Name the buyer by role and situation. If the economic buyer and the user are different people,
say which one this position addresses and what the other needs to hear.

## 2. The problem, in their voice
Write the problem as that buyer would say it, in the first person, using their vocabulary.
Draw the phrases from interviews and reviews where they exist, and mark anything invented as
an assumption to be tested.

Then state the ideal outcome from their point of view, with no mention of the product.

## 3. What is already claimed
Collect the homepage claim of every serious competitor and set them side by side. Sort them:

- **Commoditised** — everyone says it. Fast, reliable, easy, secure, AI-powered. These are
  table stakes, and claiming them harder does not work.
- **Owned** — a specific rival has made it theirs and is credible on it. Taking it means
  fighting them where they are strongest.
- **Unclaimed** — nobody is saying it. Ask why before celebrating: it may not matter to
  buyers, or it may not be deliverable.

The position worth taking is one that buyers weigh, that nobody owns, and that this product
can actually deliver.

## 4. Problem to capability to feature
Build the chain in that order, never in reverse:

1. List every problem this product solves for this buyer, in their words.
2. Group them into three to five groups.
3. Name each group as a capability described in problem terms, not technical ones. The test:
   the buyer would recognise it as their own problem being solved.
4. Map the product's features under each capability.

Any feature that maps to no capability is either not part of the position or evidence that a
problem is missing from the list. Say which.

## 5. The defensibility test
Rank the candidate claim honestly:

| Strength | What it rests on | How long it holds |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | Something a rival would have to rebuild to match: ownership, deployment model, data, a licence, a cost structure, a network | Years |
| Position | Being first to own a category or segment in buyers' minds, and scaling behind it | As long as the lead is defended |
| Feature | A capability a competent team ships in a quarter | Months |

Then ask: could a well-funded competitor copy this in one sprint? If yes, it is not a position,
it is a head start. Say so rather than dressing it up.

## 6. Write it
- **Positioning statement** — 25 words or fewer. Who it is for, the problem it removes, and why
  it is different. No formula, no buzzwords, no stacked benefits.
- **Product description** — 50 words or fewer.
- **Who this is for** — the buyer, stated narrowly.
- **Who this is not for** — stated explicitly, and it must cost something real. A "not for" list
  that excludes nobody the founder wanted anyway is decoration.

Check it against these failures before going further:

| Red flag | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Uses a competitor's name | Defensive; makes them the reference point |
| Claims two or three benefits | The reader remembers none |
| Could be said by any rival | Not a position |
| Needs a paragraph of explanation | Not a position |
| True only after the next release | Not yet true |
| "The future of", "next-generation", "revolutionary" | Says nothing, and buyers discount it |

The practical test: a stranger reads the statement and says back what the product does and who
it is for. If they cannot, rewrite it rather than explaining it.

## 7. Test it before committing
Pick the test by what exists. Signal strength, strongest first:

1. **Cold outreach replies** — real buyers with no reason to be kind. The strongest signal
   available before launch.
2. **Commitment from a buyer** — a meeting booked, a pilot discussed, a price accepted.
3. **Repeat-back** — explain the position to ten target buyers and ask them to describe it to a
   colleague. What they repeat is the position that is actually landing; what they drop is
   noise.
4. **Landing page with a real price and a real call to action** — weaker, because interest is
   not commitment.
5. **Internal agreement** — no signal at all.

Be honest about what a test can resolve. With a hundred messages per variant and a handful of
replies, only a large difference means anything; a small gap is noise, and reporting it as a
result is worse than not testing. Either raise the volume or use the test to rule out what
clearly fails, and say which of the two is happening.

Where a company already exists, change the position in stages rather than at once: test the
wording first, then align what sales and the product say, then commit publicly — and at each
stage check that existing customers still recognise who they bought from.

## 8. What would prove it wrong
Write, before testing, the result that would mean this position is wrong: the reply rate below
which the claim is not landing, the objection that would mean buyers do not weigh this
criterion, the competitor move that would make it commoditised. Decide the line in advance so
it cannot move afterwards.

## Output
Under 1000 words. Positioning is short by nature, and a long positioning document is a sign
that the position has not been found.

1. **The position** — the statement, the buyer, and who it is not for.
2. **Why this one** — the problem in the buyer's words, what is already claimed by whom, and
   the space this takes.
3. **The chain** — a table: market problems, the capability each group becomes, the features
   under it.
4. **How defensible it is** — which level, what a rival would have to do to match it, and how
   long it holds.
5. **Evidence and assumptions** — what rests on buyer evidence and what is still assumed.
6. **How to test it, and what would prove it wrong** — the test, the volume needed, and the
   line decided in advance.

## Deliverable
A Word document (.docx), built with the docx skill, named
`<product-slug>-positioning-<YYYY-MM-DD>.docx`. In the chat: the statement, who it is not for,
and the defensibility verdict.

## Rules
- Start from the problem. A position written from the feature list is rejected and rebuilt.
- Use the buyer's vocabulary. Mark any phrase not drawn from a real buyer as an assumption.
- Never claim what the product cannot deliver today.
- Never claim what every competitor also claims. Check before writing, not after.
- The "not for" must exclude someone the founder would have liked to keep.
- State the defensibility level plainly. A claim a rival can copy in a sprint is called a head
  start, not a moat.
- One buyer per position. Where several matter, write several and name the lead.
- Decide on buyer evidence, never on internal agreement, and never on how good the sentence
  sounds.
- Never report a test result that the sample size cannot support.
