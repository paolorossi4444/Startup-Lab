# Pitch

## Purpose
Make an investor able to repeat the story accurately to someone who was not in the room, and
believe it.

## Inputs
- **Required:** what the company does and what is being raised, or the intent to raise.
- **Read first where they exist:** the market sizing, the competition analysis, the positioning,
  the business model, the interview findings. The pitch assembles these. It does not produce new
  facts.
- **Missing:** say which analysis is missing and what the deck will have to assert without
  evidence because of it. Ask at most two questions, then proceed.

## How to reason
A pitch is an argument that this company could return a fund, made to someone who will hear
twenty others this month and has to justify their conviction to partners who were not present.

- **The deck is downstream of the analysis.** Every figure traces back to the work already
  done, with the same source and date. A number that appears for the first time on a slide is
  a number nobody has checked.
- **Problem before solution, always.** The customer is the one with the problem; the company is
  what lets them solve it.
- **Conviction comes from the size of the upside; urgency comes from real timing.** Both have
  to be earned, and neither can be manufactured.
- **Replace adjectives with numbers.** "Rapid growth" is an adjective. "From 4 to 31 customers
  in seven months" is an argument.
- **Say what is weak before being asked.** An investor who finds the hole themselves discounts
  everything else; a founder who names it keeps credibility.

## 1. The earned secret
State the thing this founder believes that most informed people in the market do not, and say
how they came to know it: what they saw, built, sold or lived that others have not. An insight
anyone could have read is not a secret, and a secret with no route to it is a guess.

If there is no earned secret, say so plainly rather than dressing a general observation as one.
That absence is itself the most important thing the founder could learn from this exercise.

## 2. Why now
The specific change that makes this possible or necessary now and not three years ago: a
capability that did not exist, a cost that collapsed, a rule that changed, a behaviour that
shifted. With a date and a source.

"AI is changing everything" is not a why-now. If the only answer is that the founder is ready,
say that.

## 3. The stage decides what must be proven
Name the stage and what it obliges. Each stage is judged on different evidence, and offering
the wrong kind reads as not knowing the game:

- **Pre-seed, no product:** the insight, the founders' right to it, and evidence that the
  problem is real and paid for today. Invented projections hurt here; the market sizing and the
  interview evidence carry the weight.
- **Seed:** something built, early users, and signal that they pull rather than are pushed.
  Retention and usage beat logos.
- **Series A:** repeatable acquisition and economics that hold as they scale, shown as a trend
  rather than a snapshot.

Where the evidence for the claimed stage does not exist, lower the claim rather than inflating
the evidence.

## 4. The narrative, in the order of the deck
Build the argument against the slide sequence below, then compress it. Each item is both a step
in the argument and a slide.

1. **Company purpose** — one declarative sentence saying what the company does. Not a mission,
   not a category, not a metaphor. If it takes two sentences, it is not clear yet.
2. **Problem** — the felt pain, from the customer's point of view and in their words, with
   evidence of what it costs them today.
3. **Solution** — the value proposition and the use cases it covers. What the customer can now
   do that they could not before.
4. **Why now** — the inflection point from section 2, with its date and source.
5. **Market size** — from the sizing work: TAM, SAM and SOM with both the top-down and the
   bottom-up build shown, and the assumptions visible. Never a single top-down number.
6. **Competition** — from the competition work: the honest landscape, including the status quo,
   the criteria that decide the market, and why this company wins on them. Not a feature matrix.
7. **Product** — the demo, the features that follow from the problem, and the architecture where
   it matters. Shown rather than described.
8. **Business model** — from the business-model work: how revenue is made, the price and what it
   scales with, and the unit economics with their inputs visible, lifetime value and acquisition
   cost included with the retention assumption stated.
9. **Team** — the founding story and the domain expertise: the specific thing each person has
   done before that bears on this problem.
10. **Financials** — the profit and loss, the projections and the key metrics, with the
    assumptions behind the projections stated rather than buried.

Then **the ask**, which closes the deck: the amount, what it buys, and the milestone it reaches.

Compress the whole into thirty seconds, then two minutes, then the full version. A story that
only works at full length will not survive being repeated.

## 5. The deck
The structure above is the structure of the deck, in that order. Twelve to fifteen slides for a
first meeting; anything else goes to an appendix that answers questions rather than pre-empting
them. Build the slides from the argument, never the argument from the template.

Rules that hold on every slide:

- One idea per slide. If two ideas fight, split the slide.
- The heading states the point, not the topic. "Logistics firms pay €40k a year to do this by
  hand" rather than "Problem".
- Six words per bullet, six bullets at most. Beyond that it is a document, not a slide.
- One visual per slide, and it earns its place: a line for change over time, bars for
  comparison, one large number for a single metric that matters, a simple map for competitive
  position. No pie charts, no feature matrices against competitors.
- Every figure carries its source. Uncited numbers invite the one question that derails the
  meeting.
- Speaker notes carry the argument; the slide carries the point.

## 6. The ask
Specific, and justified by what it buys: the amount, the runway it provides in months, the
milestones it reaches, and what the company will have proven by the end of it that it cannot
prove now. An amount with no plan behind it reads as a guess, and a round sized to reach
nothing in particular is the most common reason a good meeting goes nowhere.

## 7. Test it before it leaves the room
- **The heading test:** read only the slide headings, in order. If they do not tell the whole
  story on their own, the deck is a collection of slides rather than an argument.
- **The thirty-second retell:** someone who has seen it once repeats it to a colleague. What
  they get wrong is where the story is unclear; what they leave out is not carrying weight.
- **The hardest question:** write the question the founder least wants to be asked, and the
  honest answer. If the honest answer is bad, that is a problem with the company, not with the
  deck, and it is reported as such.
- **The adjective sweep:** find every adjective and either replace it with a number or cut it.
- **The believability gap:** name the claim an investor will doubt most, and what evidence would
  settle it.

## 8. What not to do
- No invented traction, no projections presented as results, and no customer logos for anyone
  who is not a customer. Say plainly which are pilots, which are conversations, and which are
  paying.
- No manufactured urgency and no invented competing interest. It is found out, and it costs
  more than the round.
- No claim the product cannot support today, for the same reason it is barred from the
  positioning.
- No business model figure that contradicts the business-model work, and no market size that
  contradicts the sizing analysis. If a bigger number is wanted, redo the
  sizing honestly or use the one that exists.

## Deliverable
Two files.

**The deck — a PowerPoint (.pptx)**, built with the pptx skill, named
`<company-slug>-pitch-<YYYY-MM-DD>.pptx`, following the ten-section structure above plus the ask,
with speaker notes under each slide and an appendix for the detail that would otherwise crowd the
main sequence.

**The narrative — a Word document (.docx)**, built with the docx skill, named
`<company-slug>-pitch-narrative-<YYYY-MM-DD>.docx`: the thirty-second version, the two-minute
version, the full argument, the hardest question with its answer, and the evidence behind each
claim with its source.

In the chat: the thirty-second version, the hardest question, and the believability gap.

## Rules
- Build only from the analyses already done. Never introduce a figure that no analysis supports.
- Every number on a slide traces to a source with a date.
- The heading of every slide states something. A heading that labels a topic is rewritten.
- Name the weakest point before an investor does.
- Where the evidence does not reach the claimed stage, lower the claim.
- Never invent traction, interest, urgency or a customer.
- If there is no earned secret and no real why-now, say so rather than manufacturing either.
