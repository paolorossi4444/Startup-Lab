# ICP and value proposition

## Purpose
Turn "our customer is mid-sized companies" into a definition specific enough to build a list
from, and a value that is stated in money rather than adjectives.

## Inputs
- **Required:** what the product does and what it replaces or improves.
- **Also read if provided:** interview findings, which carry the customer's own words and what
  they pay today; the competition analysis for who serves which segment; any existing customers
  or pipeline, which override every assumption here.
- **Missing:** ask at most two questions, then proceed with labelled assumptions.

## How to reason
An ideal customer profile is not a description of who you would like to sell to. It is a
testable claim that a specific, findable group of organisations has a problem worth money to
them, and that you can reach them.

- **Segment by what changes the value**, not by what is easy to describe. Company size matters
  only if value scales with size. If the thing that decides value is fleet size, regulatory
  regime or whether they run a night shift, segment by that.
- **The definition must be countable.** If you cannot name the source that lists these
  organisations, you do not have an ICP yet, you have an adjective.
- **Value is a number.** "Saves time" is not a value proposition. "Removes nine hours a week of
  reconciliation at a cost of €18,000 a year" is one, and it can be argued with.
- **Narrow deliberately.** A beachhead is chosen by giving things up. A segment that excludes
  nobody is the whole market with a label on it.
- **What they do beats what they are.** A trigger — a hire, a renewal, a rule change, a failure
  — predicts buying better than any attribute.

## 1. Cut the market by what moves the value
List the candidate dimensions and say which actually change what the product is worth: scale,
process, industry, geography and regulatory regime, technology in place, how they buy, how
acute the pain is, who inside holds the problem.

Choose the two or three that matter and build the segments from them. Say which dimensions you
rejected and why; rejecting the obvious ones is usually where the thinking is.

## 2. Make each segment findable
For every segment, write the attributes someone could actually filter on:

- **Firmographic**: sector classification code, size band, revenue band, legal form, locations
- **Technographic**: what they already run, which integrations that implies
- **Operational**: volumes, process in place, how the work is done today
- **Regulatory**: licences held, regimes they fall under, obligations they carry
- **Behavioural**: what they have recently done that is visible from outside

Then state the source that lists them: a business register, a regulator's published list, an
association's membership directory, a review platform's user base, a tender database, a
professional network's filters. Give the rough count from that source.

The test for this section: could someone build a list of two hundred named organisations in
this segment tomorrow, using only what is written here? If not, the definition is not finished.

## 3. Quantify the value, per segment
For each segment, build the arithmetic rather than asserting the benefit:

1. **What the problem costs them today** — hours at a loaded rate, errors at a cost per error,
   lost revenue, fines, capital tied up, a licence they pay for. With the source.
2. **What changes with the product** — the share of that cost removed, and why that share.
3. **The annual value** per customer, and the range across the segment.
4. **What they pay today** for whatever partially solves it now.

The same product is worth a different amount to each segment, and that difference is usually
the strongest reason to pick one over another. Where any step is a guess, mark it and carry it
into the test plan.

## 4. The buying unit
Name the people, because the sale is to a group:

- Who feels the pain day to day
- Who holds the budget and can sign
- Who can block it: security, legal, procurement, a union or works council
- Who inside would champion it, and what they get from it personally
- Whose job is built around the current way of doing it

State the budget line it comes from, the typical approval path, and how long it takes.

## 5. The trigger
What event makes an organisation in this segment become a buyer now rather than in two years:
a new hire in a given role, a contract renewal, a failed audit, a volume threshold crossed, a
regulation taking effect, a funding round, an incident. Say how that trigger is visible from
outside, because that is what makes outbound work.

## 6. Rank the segments and choose one
Score each segment on criteria that decide a beachhead, with the evidence behind each:

| Criterion | The question |
|---|---|
| Pain | How acute, how frequent, and does anyone own it internally |
| Value | Annual value per customer, from section 3 |
| Reachability | Can you build the list and get a meeting without a budget |
| Ability to buy | Is there a budget line and how long is the approval |
| Reference value | Do others in the market watch this group |
| Competition | Who already serves them, and how entrenched |

Then choose one to start from and say what is being given up. Rank the rest as second and third
rather than discarding them, since a beachhead is a starting point, not a permanent boundary.

## 7. Who is not a customer
The disqualification list: segments that look adjacent but are not worth pursuing, and the
specific reason for each — no budget holder, the pain is real but cheap to live with, the sales
cycle outlasts the runway, a free alternative is good enough, or an incumbent owns them.

This list has to cost something. If it excludes only groups nobody wanted, it is decoration.

## 8. The value proposition for the chosen segment
One statement, in their language, containing the number: who it is for, what it removes, what
that is worth per year, and what makes it different from what they do today. Then two or three
sentences on how the segment would describe that in their own words, drawn from interviews
where they exist and marked as invented where they do not.

Write a separate statement for the second-ranked segment if the value differs materially. The
difference between them is itself useful information.

## 9. Evidence and what to test
Mark every claim in the analysis as verified with a source, estimated with the logic shown, or
assumed. Then name the two assumptions that most need testing and the cheapest way to test
each — usually a handful of conversations with people who match the definition, which is what
the interview work exists for.

## Output
Under 1500 words.

1. **The segments** — a table: segment, defining attributes, where to find and count them, how
   many, annual value per customer.
2. **The chosen beachhead** — which one, why, and what is given up.
3. **The value, built** — the arithmetic for the chosen segment, with sources.
4. **The buying unit and the trigger.**
5. **Who is not a customer**, with the reason for each.
6. **The value proposition**, with the number in it.
7. **What rests on assumption**, and the two things to test first.

## Deliverable
A Word document (.docx), built with the docx skill, named
`<company-slug>-icp-<YYYY-MM-DD>.docx`. In the chat: the chosen segment in one line with its
countable definition, the annual value per customer, and the first thing to test.

Hand the countable definition to the market sizing, which cannot count buyers without it.

## Rules
- The ICP must be countable and findable. Name the source that lists these organisations and
  the rough count, or say plainly that the definition is not finished.
- Segment by what changes the value, never by what is convenient to describe. Say which
  dimensions were rejected.
- State value as money per customer per year, with the arithmetic and the sources. Adjectives
  are not a value proposition.
- Name the budget holder. A segment where nobody can sign is not a segment.
- The disqualification list must exclude something the founder wanted.
- Choose one beachhead and say what it costs to choose it.
- Mark every claim verified, estimated or assumed, and never present a guessed value as a
  finding.
