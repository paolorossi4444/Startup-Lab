# User interviews

## Purpose
Turn "talk to users" into a process that produces evidence, and read the results honestly.

## Three modes
Work out which one the request calls for, and say which you are in.

- **Prepare** — who to talk to, how to reach them, what to ask.
- **Rehearse** — play the customer so the founder can practise, then critique the questions.
- **Synthesise** — read the real notes and say what they show.

## Inputs
- **Required:** what the founder wants to learn, or the notes to be read.
- **Also read if provided:** an earlier idea review and its riskiest assumption, which is
  usually what the interviews exist to test. If one exists, say which assumption each
  question is there to attack.
- **Missing:** ask at most two questions, then proceed with a stated choice.

## Prepare

### 1. What has to be settled
Name the one or two beliefs these conversations must break or support, and the specific thing
that would count as either. Interviews without that written down produce pleasant
conversations and no decision.

### 2. Who to talk to
Define the person by what they do, not by a demographic: the role, the situation, and the
recent event that means they have this problem now. Then set a target: how many, split across
which segments.

Warn against the easy sample. Friends, people from the founder's own company and anyone who
wants to be encouraging will distort the result. Say how many of the interviews should come
from people with no reason to be kind.

### 3. Where to find them and how to reach them
Work out the actual channels for this specific population, which differ every time:
professional networks and search filters, industry associations and member directories,
trade events and conferences, sector communities and forums, review sites where they
complain about the tools they already use, public procurement records, warm introductions,
and the founder's own second-degree network.

Then write the outreach: short, specific, honest about who the founder is, asking for a small
and defined amount of time, and explicitly not selling anything. Give a version for each
channel being used, and a follow-up. State a realistic response rate so the founder knows how
many to send.

### 4. The question guide
Ten to fifteen questions, ordered from broad to specific, built on these rules:

- Ask about what happened, not what would happen. The last time this problem came up, what
  did they do? What did it cost them? What did they try before that?
- Never describe the idea before the questions. A pitch turns an interview into a courtesy.
- Follow the money and the time: what they already pay for, what they built internally, who
  signs off, what budget it came from.
- Ask for specifics, and if an answer is general, ask for the last instance of it.
- Avoid questions that can be answered yes. "Would you use this", "do you think this is a
  good idea" and "would you pay for this" produce agreement and no information.
- End by asking for something real: an introduction, a follow-up with a colleague who holds
  the budget, data, a pilot. What they agree to give is worth more than anything they said.

For each question, write in one line what you learn from it and which belief it attacks.
Add the follow-up probes to use when an answer is vague.

## Rehearse
Play a specific person from the target list — one situation, one set of constraints, not a
generic customer — and answer as that person plausibly would, including being busy,
sceptical, or wrong about their own behaviour. Stay in character until the founder stops.

Then step out and critique: which questions led the witness, which invited a yes, which
produced a hypothetical instead of a memory, and what to ask instead. Rewrite the weakest
three.

**Simulated answers are never evidence.** Say so every time. Nothing from a rehearsal goes
into a synthesis, a market sizing, or any other analysis.

## Synthesise
Read the real notes. Work bottom-up from what people actually said before forming any view.

1. **Who was spoken to:** how many, which segments, how they were found, and who is missing
   from the sample. Name the bias the sample has.
2. **What was said, in their words:** group the verbatim phrases by theme. Quote exactly,
   because the founder's paraphrase is already an interpretation. Keep the phrases people use
   for the problem — they are what the product and the copy should later say.
3. **Said against did:** for each theme, separate what people claimed from what they have
   actually done — switched, paid, built a workaround, assigned someone to it. Count both.
4. **Who disagreed:** report the people who contradicted the hypothesis first, before the
   supporting evidence, and in the same detail.
5. **Commitments obtained:** introductions, follow-ups, data, pilots, money. List them. If
   none were obtained across many conversations, say plainly that enthusiasm without
   commitment is the normal shape of a problem nobody will pay to solve.
6. **The verdict on each belief:** supported, contradicted, or still open — and for the open
   ones, what would settle it.
7. **What this cannot tell you:** the questions these interviews did not cover, and the
   sample size caveat. Five to ten conversations give direction, not a pattern; patterns need
   more, and the number depends on how varied the segment is.

Rank the strength of what you have: behaviour and commitment outweigh promises, which
outweigh opinions, which outweigh compliments. Compliments are not data and are reported as
noise.

## Deliverable
A Word document (.docx), built with the docx skill.

- **Prepare:** `<idea-slug>-interview-guide-<YYYY-MM-DD>.docx` — targets, channels, outreach
  messages, the question guide with its purpose column.
- **Synthesise:** `<idea-slug>-interview-findings-<YYYY-MM-DD>.docx` — the sections above.
- **Rehearse:** no file; it happens in the conversation.

In the chat: for a guide, the target and the first three questions; for a synthesis, the
verdict on each belief and the commitments obtained.

## Rules
- Never write a question that can be answered with a yes, or that asks about the future.
- Never let the founder pitch before the questions. If the request is for an interview script
  that explains the product first, say why that destroys the result and offer the alternative.
- Quote people exactly. Never improve, shorten or smooth a verbatim phrase.
- Report the people who disagreed before the people who agreed.
- A compliment is not evidence, an opinion about the future is not evidence, and a simulated
  answer is never evidence. Say it when it comes up.
- Do not conclude beyond the sample. State the number of conversations next to any pattern
  claimed.
- Never invent a quote, a respondent or a number. If the notes are thin, say what is missing.
