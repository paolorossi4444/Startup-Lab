# Business model

## Purpose
Decide how this company makes money, and say honestly whether the economics work or what would
have to be true for them to.

## Inputs
- **Required:** what the product does and for whom.
- **Read first where they exist:** the competition analysis for what rivals charge and how,
  the market sizing for the value per customer already estimated, interview findings for what
  buyers pay today and who signs.
- **If there is revenue already:** actual prices, contracts, churn and cost data override every
  assumption below.
- **Missing:** ask at most two questions, then proceed with labelled assumptions.

## How to reason
The business model is not a label like "SaaS" or "marketplace". It is a set of linked choices
about who pays, for what unit, how often, at what cost to serve — and the test is whether those
choices still work at scale, not whether they can be made to work on one deal.

- **Follow the money, not the product.** Who has the budget, who signs, and what line it comes
  out of decide more than the feature set.
- **Price is evidence of value, not a parameter to tune.** Start from what the problem costs
  the customer today and what they already pay to reduce it.
- **Gross margin is where the truth lives.** It is where a services business dressed as
  software is exposed, and no growth rate repairs a structurally thin margin.
- **Every ratio needs its inputs shown.** A lifetime value built on invented retention is a
  number with a decimal point and nothing behind it.
- **Benchmark against what comparable businesses actually achieve**, from filings and
  disclosures, not against what the category is said to deliver.
- **Say what breaks it.** Name the assumption that, if wrong by half, makes the model
  unviable.

## When the evidence is thin
Much of what follows may rest on nothing yet, and that is normal early. Where it does, the
answer gets shorter, not longer and more hedged. Say in the first line how much of this rests
on evidence and how much on reasoning. Reduce any unsupported section to its conclusion and the
one thing that would settle it. Put what to go and find out at the top rather than at the end.
A short honest answer is worth more than a long one padded to look complete.

## 1. Who pays, who uses, who decides
Name them separately, because they are often different people:
- The **user** who gets the benefit
- The **economic buyer** who holds the budget
- The **approver** who can block it, often security, legal, procurement or a works council

State which budget line this comes from and what it displaces, because money spent here is
money not spent somewhere else and someone will defend that.

## 2. How value is created and what share can be captured
State in one line what the customer gains: time saved, revenue gained, loss avoided, risk
removed, a cost replaced. Quantify it per customer per year where possible, with the source.

Then state the share of that value this company can realistically take, and why. Capturing a
large share of a measurable gain is possible where the gain is attributable and the alternative
is expensive; it is not where the benefit is diffuse or where a free alternative exists.

## 3. The revenue model
Choose from what actually fits how value arrives, and say why the others do not:

| Model | Fits when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription | Value is continuous and the customer needs it available | Churn, and the gap between seats sold and seats used |
| Usage-based | Value tracks volume and volume is measurable | Unpredictable bills, which procurement resists |
| Transaction or take rate | The company sits in the flow of money or goods | Disintermediation once both sides know each other |
| Licence | Deployment is on the customer's own infrastructure | Upgrade cycles, and support costs that are not priced in |
| Services and implementation | The work is bespoke and expert-led | Margin, and that it does not scale without hiring |
| Marketplace | Both sides are fragmented and discovery is hard | Having to solve supply and demand at the same time |
| Outcome-based | The result is measurable and attributable to the product | Attribution disputes, and carrying the customer's risk |

Most real models are a mixture. Say which part is which, and what share of revenue each carries
at maturity, because a mixture of 80% services and 20% software is a services business.

## 4. The value metric
Pick the thing the price scales with. A good value metric rises as the customer gets more
value, is predictable enough for them to budget, and is not something they will work to avoid.

Test the candidate against all three. Charging per seat when value comes from volume means
customers share logins; charging per query when the customer cannot forecast queries means
procurement blocks the deal.

## 5. The price
- **What they pay today** for the alternative, including internal labour, with the source.
- **The price range**, built from that rather than from competitors alone, with the logic shown.
- **Packaging**: what sits in each tier and what moves the customer up one.
- **Evidence**: what any of this rests on. A price nobody has been quoted is an assumption, and
  is labelled as one.

If there is no evidence of willingness to pay, say so and treat it as the first thing to test,
not as something to assume.

## 6. Unit economics
Define the unit first — a customer, an account, a transaction, a site — then build:

- **Revenue per unit per year**, from the price and the expected usage
- **Cost to serve**: infrastructure, third-party fees, support, implementation, the share of
  people time this consumes. Then the **gross margin** it implies
- **Acquisition cost**, by channel, including the founder's own time valued honestly
- **Payback period**: months of gross profit to repay acquisition
- **Retention**, stated as an assumption with its basis, and the **lifetime value** that
  follows, shown as a range rather than a point

Pre-revenue, say plainly that retention is unknown and give the range from comparable
businesses rather than a single confident figure. Ratios of lifetime value to acquisition cost
mean little before any cohort has had time to churn; report the inputs and let them be judged.

## 7. The cash cycle and what it takes to start
- When money is received against when it is paid out: upfront annual billing is a different
  business from monthly billing with net-60 terms.
- Working capital tied up in inventory, float, prepaid capacity or receivables.
- Upfront investment before the first revenue, and what it buys.

A model that works on paper and needs twelve months of cash before the first invoice is a
different proposition, and the analysis says so rather than leaving it in a footnote.

## 8. What it looks like at scale
Sketch the shape at maturity: gross margin, the cost structure once acquisition stops growing,
and what has to be true for the business to reach it. State the gross margin this model can
structurally reach, which is set by how the value is delivered and cannot be fixed later by
volume.

## 9. Benchmarks
Compare the key figures — gross margin, acquisition payback, net retention, revenue per
employee — against businesses with a similar delivery model, taken from filings and
prospectuses rather than from category averages. Report where this model sits, including when
it sits outside the range, and say why.

## 10. What has to be true
Close with the two or three assumptions the model depends on, each with its current evidence
and the cheapest way to test it. Name the one that, if it is half as good as assumed, makes the
business unviable.

## Output
Under 1500 words.

1. **The model in three lines** — who pays, for what unit, how often, and the gross margin it
   implies.
2. **Why this model and not the others** — the alternatives considered and rejected, with
   reasons.
3. **The economics** — a table of inputs and results, each marked verified, estimated or
   assumed, with sources and dates.
4. **Against comparables** — where these figures sit relative to similar businesses.
5. **The cash picture** — cycle, working capital, what it takes to start.
6. **What has to be true** — the dependent assumptions, the evidence, and the test for each.

## Deliverable
Two files.

**The model (.xlsx)**, built with the xlsx skill: inputs on their own tab and clearly separated
from calculations, every assumption in its own cell with a note on where it came from, scenarios
for pessimistic, base and optimistic, and the outputs on one summary tab.

**The memo (.docx)**, built with the docx skill, named
`<company-slug>-business-model-<YYYY-MM-DD>.docx`, carrying the six sections above.

In the chat: the model in three lines, the gross margin, and the assumption that breaks it.

## Rules
- Never present a lifetime value, or a ratio built on one, without showing the retention
  assumption and its basis. Give a range, not a point.
- Never report a ratio as healthy by a rule of thumb. Report the inputs and judge them.
- State the structural gross margin honestly. A business that is mostly services is called a
  services business whatever the product is named.
- Separate price from evidence of willingness to pay, and label which is which.
- Value the founder's own time in acquisition cost. Free labour hides an unviable model.
- Benchmark against filings, not against category folklore. Cite what you use.
- Name the assumption that breaks the model. An analysis with no breaking point was not tested.
